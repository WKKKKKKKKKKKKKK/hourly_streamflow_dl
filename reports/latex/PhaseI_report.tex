\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Hourly streamflow models are trained where hourly discharge is recorded, which is a small
and geographically narrow part of the world. This report asks whether hourly skill survives
at gauges that supply only daily totals. A fifth of the network is held out, its hourly
observations are hidden, and only the 24-hour aggregate supervises it; the hidden hourly
series is used once, to score. Part~I states what was run and why each step was necessary;
Part~II reports what came out, section for section in the same order.
\end{abstract}

\section{Configuration and data path}\label{sec:e-config}
Two design choices had to be settled before any result could be attributed to the method rather than to the setup.

\textbf{How the daily branch is built.} Run~A feeds it a power-law subsample of the hourly record; run~B feeds it 365 genuine daily means. These are different inputs of different lengths, not a tuning knob, so both were run to completion rather than one being chosen in advance.

\textbf{Look-back and forget gate.} v1 used a \SI{72}{\hour} hourly look-back and PyTorch's default forget-gate initialisation; v2 uses \SI{336}{\hour} and an initial forget bias of 3. The forget gate is part of the published method this model follows and was absent from v1 by oversight: at the default the effective forget gate is 0.500, a memory of about two steps, against 0.953 and about twenty-one steps at bias~3. v3 repeats v2 with 50 epochs and patience 10, purely to check that v2 was not stopped prematurely. All eleven combinations were run and all are reported.

\section{The task, the network, and the main comparison}\label{sec:e-setup}
Hourly streamflow models are trained where hourly discharge is recorded, which is a small and geographically narrow part of the world. The question is whether hourly skill survives at gauges that supply only daily totals. The premise is made testable by withholding: a fifth of the gauges are held out, their hourly observations hidden, and only the 24-hour aggregate used to supervise them. The hidden hourly series is then used once, to score.

The model is an sMTS-LSTM with two branches: the daily branch reads the past 365 days and hands its state to the hourly branch, which reads the last \SI{336}{\hour}. Three states are distinguished throughout:
\begin{description}
  \item[M0] zero-shot -- pretrained on the source gauges, evaluated on the held-out target gauges without ever seeing them.
  \item[M1] after fine-tuning on the target gauges' \emph{daily aggregates} only.
  \item[STEP 3] the source domain re-scored afterwards, to check what the adaptation cost where the model already worked.
\end{description}

A five-fold design gives every one of the 8,979 gauges exactly one turn as a target gauge, so the target-domain scores cover the whole network rather than a fifth of it and no result depends on which fifth was drawn.

\section{Why a daily total should help at all}\label{sec:e-mechanism}
A 24-hour total carries how much water arrived, not which hour it arrived in. If that is the whole of what it contributes, then fine-tuning on it should repair the amplitude and volume terms of KGE and leave the correlation term largely alone. That is a falsifiable prediction, so KGE was decomposed into $r$, $\alpha=\sigma_{\text{sim}}/\sigma_{\text{obs}}$ and $\beta=\mu_{\text{sim}}/\mu_{\text{obs}}$ per gauge, and the same decomposition was repeated on the African catchments, whose zero-shot deficits are about four times larger. A mechanism that only holds on one domain is a description.

\section{Could it be gaming the aggregate loss?}\label{sec:e-degenerate}
The aggregate loss constrains only the mean of 24 hourly outputs, so a model emitting a constant value within each day would satisfy it perfectly and be useless hourly. Stride-24 sampling makes each sample's last 24 outputs one calendar day and consecutive days stitch into a continuous series, so within-day variability can be measured directly against the observations. A sharper form of the same question was added later: averaging every day by hour of day destroys event-driven structure, so whatever survives that average is tied to the clock rather than to rainfall.

\section{Could it be a metric artefact?}\label{sec:e-metrics}
KGE and point-wise absolute error can move in opposite directions on the same gauge. Both were computed per gauge so the disagreement could be quantified rather than assumed away, and per-gauge significance was tested with a paired Wilcoxon test under Benjamini--Hochberg control.

\section{Could it be a training-budget artefact?}\label{sec:e-convergence}
If v2 had simply run out of epochs, its gain would be an artefact of the budget. v3's longer budget answers that. The same run also measures a cost the method imposes on itself: the checkpoint has to be selected on a daily-aggregate criterion, because the hourly truth is supposed to be hidden, and the difference against selecting on the hidden hourly truth is what daily-only model selection costs.

\section{Where does the gain land?}\label{sec:e-strata}
Per-gauge gains were stratified by catchment area, by distance to the nearest trainable gauge, by agency and by latitude band. The purpose is a boundary, not a headline: a method that works on average and nowhere in particular is not usable. The isolation stratification is also a check on the previous section -- if the gain were driven by proximity to training gauges, it would fall with distance.

\section{What does spatial blocking cost?}\label{sec:e-split}
The held-out fifth can be drawn at random or in spatial blocks. Random splitting leaves target gauges with trainable neighbours a median of \SI{10.4}{\km} away; \num{120} k-means blocks on the sphere push that to \SI{94.9}{\km}. Both were run with everything else identical, because the difference measures how much of the zero-shot skill is genuine generalisation and how much is proximity. Fold-to-fold dispersion was recorded for both, which turned out to matter as much as the level.

\section{An external test, daily}\label{sec:e-africa-daily}
Everything above simulates the premise: the hourly data exists and is withheld. Africa does not need the simulation. Of the African entries in the daily database only \num{302} carry a discharge time series at all and \num{294} of those are used here -- the test set of a published continent-holdout run, adopted verbatim so the numbers sit directly against its baseline. Not one appears anywhere in training and not one has hourly discharge; \num{282} are scored after a \num{100}-day minimum.

The same pretrained models are driven by hourly ERA5-Land forcing, their last 24 hourly outputs averaged to a daily value and compared with observed daily discharge; fine-tuning then uses African daily observations themselves. ERA5-Land's own runoff is carried alongside as a physical baseline, re-scored on the identical basin-days, because the per-basin scores that already existed came from a different run over a different period and would not have been comparable.

\section{The same test, hourly}\label{sec:e-africa-hourly}
No African catchment has hourly discharge, so the hourly output there can be compared between series but never scored. Two questions remain askable. First, whether the hourly output carries sub-daily structure at all, which is measured as the within-day coefficient of variation over every basin. Second, whether that structure is of the right kind -- event-driven rather than clock-driven -- which is answered on the target domain, where hourly observations exist and the observed average day can be measured directly.

\part{Results}

\section{Only one data path gains, and the forget gate decides how much}\label{sec:r-config}
Run~A moves \emph{backwards} under both configurations: its median target-domain hourly KGE falls from \num{0.427} to \num{0.397} under v1 and from \num{0.424} to \num{0.397} under v2. A power-law subsample of the hourly record is not a daily branch, and no amount of fine-tuning repairs that. Every run~B variant gains.

Within run~B the forget gate is what separates a modest result from a clear one. Under v1 the blocked split reaches \num{0.475}; under v2 it reaches \num{0.621}. v3's longer budget changes almost nothing (\num{0.624} blocked, \num{0.627} true daily), which is the first evidence that v2 was not simply stopped early. v2 is the primary configuration throughout.

Within run~B, v1 to v2 -- the forget-gate initialisation and the longer hourly look-back -- is what lifts the blocked split from \num{0.475} to \num{0.621}; v3's extra epochs add nothing.}

\section{The main result, and the shape of the network it rests on}\label{sec:r-main}
Under v2 with a random split, median target-domain hourly KGE rises from \num{0.5190} at M0 to \num{0.6210} at M1, a median change of \num{+0.0623} over 8,979 gauges. \SI{68}{\percent} of gauges improve, which is the part that matters: the gain is broad rather than an average pulled up by a few. Under the blocked split the same fine-tuning lifts \num{0.4185} to \num{0.6165}.

The map that shows this also shows its limit. The gauge network is CAMELSH \num{5115}, BOMAustralia \num{1804}, LamaHCE \num{834}, Japan \num{690}, Germany \num{463}, LamaHIce \num{73}. There is not one gauge in Africa, South America or mainland Asia, and roughly four fifths sit between \SI{30}{\degree} and \SI{60}{\degree}~N. ``Global'' describes the model, not the gauge network, which is why the African test in Section~\ref{sec:r-africa-daily} cannot be substituted for.

\section{The gain is a variance repair, not a timing repair}\label{sec:r-mechanism}
Decomposing KGE separates the claim. Correlation, which carries timing, moves by \num{+0.0054} (\num{0.797} to \num{0.812}). The variability ratio $\alpha=\sigma_{\text{sim}}/\sigma_{\text{obs}}$ moves by \num{+0.0370}, and the bias ratio $\beta$ moves from \num{1.025} toward \num{0.996}. A daily total carries how much water, not which hour it arrived, so this is what it should repair -- and it is a falsifiable prediction, not a description.

\section{Not a degenerate solution, and not over-dispersed either}\label{sec:r-degenerate}
A model gaming the aggregate loss would flatten the day. It does not: within-day variability survives, and under v2 it is neither suppressed nor exaggerated.

A sharper version of the same question: is the hourly output responding to rainfall events, or echoing the clock? Averaging every day by hour of day destroys event-driven structure, so what survives is systematic. The observed average day has a peak-to-trough ratio of \num{1.044} and the model's \num{1.098} -- the real river's average day is nearly flat, so near-flat is the correct answer for catchments of this size, and the model is \num{1.05}$\times$ the observed amplitude: marginally more variable than reality, not less.

\section{The two metrics disagree on a third of gauges}\label{sec:r-metrics}
KGE and point-wise absolute error agree on about \SI{68}{\percent} of gauges and disagree on the rest: roughly a fifth improve on KGE while their point-wise error worsens, and an eighth do the reverse. This is a consequence of the mechanism rather than a contradiction of it -- restoring variance moves peaks, which improves the shape metrics and can increase squared error at individual hours. A per-gauge claim must therefore say which metric it is made under.

\end{figure}

\section{Training was long enough, and daily-only selection is nearly free}\label{sec:r-convergence}
v2 ended on early stopping, not on its epoch cap, in every fold -- so the gain is not a budget artefact. The blocked split stopped earliest, which is consistent with its noisier validation signal rather than with worse convergence. Selecting the checkpoint on a daily-aggregate criterion instead of on the hidden hourly truth costs \num{0.0035} in median KGE, against a fold-to-fold noise level of \num{0.0078}: the cost of the method's own model-selection constraint is smaller than the noise it is measured against.

\section{Where the gain lands}\label{sec:r-strata}
Small catchments gain most (Spearman $\rho=-0.17$ against area), which fits the mechanism: a small fast-responding catchment is where a zero-shot model is least calibrated and where a daily total adds most information. Isolation does \emph{not} reduce the gain -- under the blocked split it rises with distance to the nearest trainable gauge -- which is the opposite of what a proximity-driven result would do.

\section{What spatial blocking costs, and how much fine-tuning returns}\label{sec:r-split}
Blocking the split costs the zero-shot model a paired median of \num{-0.0594} in KGE, which is \SI{11.0}{\percent} of its random-split level, with \SI{63.7}{\percent} of gauges worse and Wilcoxon $p=\num{1.6e-168}$. The drop is negative in all six agencies, so it is not one region's peculiarity. Daily-aggregate fine-tuning then returns \SI{89.7}{\percent} of it: the residual at M1 is only \num{-0.0061}.

A second, less comfortable finding sits beside it. Random splitting does not only overstate the \emph{level} of the result, it overstates its \emph{precision}: the fold-to-fold standard deviation of M1 is \num{0.0035} under the random split against \num{0.0411} under the blocked one, a factor of \num{11.8} (Levene $p=\num{0.034}$). Near-duplicate gauges on both sides of a random split mean its validation metric averages over fewer independent catchments than its gauge count suggests. The honest statement of the blocked result is ``about $0.62\pm0.04$'', not ``$0.628\pm0.004$''.

\section{Africa, daily: the premise occurring naturally}\label{sec:r-africa-daily}
On \num{282} African catchments that appear nowhere in training and have no hourly discharge at all, the zero-shot model reaches a median KGE of \num{0.1143}. Fine-tuning on African daily observations alone lifts it to \num{0.5761}, a paired median change of \num{+0.4094} with \SI{87.6}{\percent} of basins improving.

Africa is also where the timing claim of Section~\ref{sec:r-mechanism} stops holding as stated. Everywhere else $r$ barely moves because it was already right; in Africa it starts genuinely broken and does move. That is not a contradiction but a boundary: a daily total fixes which \emph{day} the water arrives, and only a domain whose day-scale timing is wrong can show it.

\section{Africa, hourly: what can and cannot be said}\label{sec:r-africa-hourly}
The hourly output is not a flattened daily mean. Over all \num{284} African basins the within-day coefficient of variation is \num{0.1246} at M0 and \num{0.1053} at M1, neither near zero.

But daily-only supervision does measurably flatten it. The paired median change is \num{-0.0192}, about \SI{15}{\percent} of M0's value, at Wilcoxon $p=\num{1.7e-04}$, and within-day variation rises in only \SI{40}{\percent} of basins. This is a cost of the method that had not previously been measured.

Whether that flattening loses \emph{real} structure cannot be settled in Africa, because no hourly observation exists there. On the target domain, where it does, the same step moved within-day standard deviation from \num{0.86} to \num{0.89} of observed -- toward the observations, not away.

ERA5-Land is drawn beside the model in the hourly panels as a contrast and not as a reference. It has no river routing, so its basin average is runoff generation leaving the soil column rather than water passing a gauge: on \texttt{restricted\_ADHI\_\_258} its instantaneous rate reaches \SI{199}{\mm\per\day} where the daily observation peaks near \num{20}, while its daily mean matches (\SI{7.75}{\mm\per\day} against an observed \num{7.58}). The volume is close; the distribution inside the day is not. Its average day swings by a factor of \num{4.2} with a 15:00~UTC peak -- afternoon convective rainfall passed straight through -- while the model damps the rainfall's clock-driven cycle \num{4.6}-fold across all \num{284} basins.

\section{What Phase~I establishes, and what it does not}\label{sec:r-summary}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Daily-aggregate supervision recovers most of the hourly skill lost to withholding hourly data, on \num{8843} gauges and on \num{282} African catchments that were never in training.
  \item The repair is of magnitude, not of sub-daily timing, and that prediction holds across two domains four times apart in deficit scale.
  \item It is not a degenerate solution, not a metric artefact and not a training-budget artefact; each was tested separately.
  \item Under-dispersion is reduced, not removed: \SI{74}{\percent} of gauges remain under-dispersed after fine-tuning, so this does not yet deliver a model to be trusted on peak magnitude.
  \item Random splitting overstates both the level and the precision of the result. Blocked-split numbers, with their honest fold-to-fold spread, are the ones to quote.
  \item The gauge network is temperate and Northern-Hemisphere; ``global'' describes the model. The African test is the only genuinely external evidence here and it rests on \num{302} records, which is all the daily database holds for the continent.
\end{itemize}
